# Source Credibility Guide

How to decide whether a source is good enough to rely on. These criteria are inspired by the ones librarians teach (such as the CRAAP test: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) and the kind of credibility hints citation tools give. This guide is not a copy of any tool's scoring method.

Judge the **specific page or document**, not only the website. A good publisher can host a weak opinion piece, and a weak site can quote a strong primary source (in which case, go cite the primary source).

## The six questions

For each source, ask:

1. **Who wrote it?** Is there a named author, and are they qualified on this topic (credentials, institution, track record)? No author at all is a red flag.
2. **Who published it, and why?** Government agency, journal, university, news organization, company, advocacy group, individual? Is the goal to inform, to sell, or to persuade?
3. **Is it checked?** Peer review, editorial review, fact-checking, a published corrections policy. Content that went out with no one checking it is weaker.
4. **Does it show its evidence?** Does it link to data, studies, or primary documents? Can you follow the claim back to where it started?
5. **When was it published or updated?** Is it recent enough for this question? A 2015 statistic may be fine for history and useless for "current" figures.
6. **Is there a conflict of interest?** A company's claims about its own product, a group funded by an interested industry, or affiliate-link "reviews" need independent confirmation.

## Tiers

### Tier 1: strong (can stand alone for the claim it directly covers)
- Government and intergovernmental agencies on their own data (e.g. national statistics offices, CDC, NASA, WHO, UN agencies)
- Peer-reviewed journal articles (check that the journal is real and not a predatory publisher)
- Court records, legislation, official filings (e.g. SEC filings)
- Primary documents: the original report, transcript, dataset, or the organization's official statement about itself (for facts *about* that organization)

### Tier 2: good
- Established news organizations with editorial standards and a corrections policy
- University and research institution pages
- Recognized professional and expert bodies (medical associations, standards organizations)
- Reputable reference works (major encyclopedias, academic handbooks)
- Books from established academic or major publishers

### Tier 3: use with care (good for leads; trace back to Tier 1–2 when possible)
- Wikipedia: great for finding sources; cite what *its* references say, after checking them
- Trade and industry publications
- Blogs and newsletters by identifiable experts
- Video and podcasts from identifiable institutions or experts (see below)
- Preprints (not yet peer reviewed; say so if you use them)

### Don't rely on
- Anonymous or unsigned content with no sourcing
- Content farms, SEO listicles, and "top 10" pages written to rank in search
- Sites selling the product they review, and affiliate-link review sites
- Forums, Q&A sites, comment sections, and social media posts (they may point you to a real source, but they aren't the source)
- Pages that look AI-generated: generic text, no author, no dates, no citations
- Known satire sites and known misinformation sites
- Predatory journals and "papers" with no real publisher

## Video (YouTube etc.)

Judge the channel and the speaker:

| Who uploaded it | How to treat it |
|---|---|
| University, government agency, museum, or major research org (official channel) | Tier 2 for what the speaker presents |
| News organization's own channel | Same tier as that outlet's written work |
| Named expert speaking in their field (conference talk, lecture) | Tier 2–3 |
| Company's official channel, about itself | Tier 1 for its own announcements; biased on anything else |
| Popular explainer or education channel | Tier 3 at best; check whether it cites sources in the description |
| Anonymous channel, reaction video, reupload, compilation | Don't rely on it |

Video should rarely be the only support for an important claim. Look for the written source it's based on.

## Red flags on any source
- Emotional or sensational headline that the body doesn't back up
- Statistics with no source, or a source you can't find
- Quotes with no context, date or link to the original
- Only one site reports something that would be big news if true
- The URL imitates a known outlet (e.g. "abcnews.com.co")
- Claims that a source says something it doesn't (always check the original)

## Independence check

Before counting two sources as separate confirmation, check that one isn't just repeating the other. Many articles rewrite the same press release, wire story, or single study. If they all trace back to one origin, that's one source, and you should judge that origin.
